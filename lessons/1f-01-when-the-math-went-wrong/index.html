When the math went wrong

Long before ChatGPT, scoring systems quietly ranked people for loans, jobs and prison sentences. Some made unfairness bigger.

How it works

1

A score is built from the past.

A model learns from old records: who paid back loans, who stayed in jobs, who was arrested again. If the past was unfair, the pattern it learns is unfair too.

How it works

2

The score drives a decision.

A low score can mean a loan turned down, a résumé never read, or a longer sentence suggested to a judge. Often the person never learns the score existed.

How it works

3

The decision shapes what happens next.

Fewer chances lead to worse outcomes. Turned down for credit, people borrow somewhere more expensive, and their record gets worse.

How it works

4

The new record proves the score.

The bad outcome becomes fresh data, and the model looks more right than ever. The mathematician Cathy O’Neil called systems like this “weapons of math destruction.”

Good to know

Her three warning signs

The system is secret, so people can’t see how it works; it’s big, so it touches many lives; and it does damage that nobody fixes.

What experience teaches

A system is only as fair as the history it learned from.

Today’s chatbots learned from human writing, with all its slants. Hiring tools, insurance prices and credit checks increasingly use AI too. When a decision about you comes from a system, ask two questions: what was it trained on, and who checks it?

A good system has answers. A bad one hides behind “the computer decided.”

Try this · 1 of 2

Ask the two questions

I was turned down for [a loan, an apartment or a job] and I think a computer system was involved. What questions can I ask to find out how the decision was made, and what might my rights be?

You’ll see questions to ask and places to start. Confirm your rights with an official source where you live.

The catch

Not every score is a weapon. Credit scores, for example, are regulated in many places, and you can usually see and dispute your report. The book came out in 2016; some systems have improved since, and others have grown.

The point isn’t to fear every algorithm. It’s to ask who checks it.

Remember

A system is only as fair as the history it learned from. Ask what it was trained on, and who checks it.
